A new owner flew me and a few support crew down to take over her newly purchased first yacht. Before we even reached the boat, the tone was already starting to set. We boarded her private jet, and the engineer had turned the cabin of a Lear 75 into a traveling tool crib, with toolboxes stacked down the center aisle.

We landed in West Palm Beach, took an Uber to Palm Harbor Marina, and expected to board the boat and head more or less straight for Fort Lauderdale. The seller had already cancelled the slip, so the plan on paper was simple: get aboard, do the basics, and move the boat south to the yard for assessment and refit.

When I first stepped aboard, the boat did not feel like a lost cause. It felt like it had once been a good boat. But sometime after the original crew was fired and the boat was put on the market, it had spent more than a year sitting without real crew aboard while a management company allegedly looked after it, billed for it, and mostly ignored it. It had that particular feeling some boats get when the bones are still there but the stewardship has gone missing.

Then we found a stereo crew already aboard.

Apparently the owner had decided the most urgent improvement this newly purchased yacht needed before heading to the yard for assessment was an upgraded exterior sound system. Contracts had already been signed. Nobody had mentioned that little detail to me in advance.

That was the first real red flag.

We waited for the stereo installers to pack up while we started pre-departure checks. Then the boat began telling the truth. Only one generator would start. The pilothouse engine controls did not work. That left us with only the flybridge and wing station controls available. Then, just to improve the mood, the insurance company informed us that because of the survey report the boat was effectively under harbor arrest until several issues were corrected.

So the simple move to Fort Lauderdale was starting to look a lot more educational than advertised.

To his credit, the engineer went to work and started grinding through problems. Between his repairs and our diagnosis, we reached the point where we believed the boat could safely make the run south under its own power. So with a flare start, we left.

For a while, it actually went smoothly.

Then as we entered Port Everglades, the insurance representative called and told me that because the pilothouse controls were inoperative, we would need to be towed up the New River. Given where we were and the time of day, I told them we would be much better off stopping at a marina on the ICW and arranging a tug in the morning. That seemed like the obviously sane answer.

Instead, I was told we were only cleared to the yard upriver and should go ahead and drive up the river in the dark with questionable controls and only one generator running.

Which was an impressive recommendation.

Fortunately, a few hundred yards past the river entrance, several alarms started going off while we were right in front of Bahia Mar. We treated that as the gift that it was and tied up on the face dock to sort out what turned out to be a very benign alarm. Once we were secure, another call to the insurance company produced a much more rational answer. Suddenly they were perfectly happy for us to stay put and get tugged upriver in the morning.

Then, because some stories insist on making their point twice, the stereo guys showed up bright and early the next day to keep working. The boat might not run properly, but at least it was going to sound fantastic.

That first trip told me a lot about what kind of chapter that boat was going to be. Before we had even made it to the yard, the priorities were already clear. Mechanical soundness could wait. Appearances, or at least the party version of appearances, apparently could not.

Exterior presentation tells you something about a program, but it does not tell you everything. A neglected exterior often does foreshadow what you are about to find inside, but even that is not always reliable. Sometimes a rough-looking boat surprises you. And sometimes a perfectly manicured yacht tied up at the dock, polished and shining and giving every outward sign of competence, is already trying to tell you something is wrong the moment you step aboard.

Usually it starts with smell.

If the interior smells like mold, blackwater, stale humidity, or some other version of trapped system failure, that is the first sign. Then you start hearing things. A pump laboring. A fan motor struggling. An air handler that sounds tired. Lights dimming when they should not. A toilet flush that drags voltage down like the boat is negotiating with itself.

To owners and guests, that world is often completely foreign. They may assume all boats smell that way. They may assume everybody gets bronchitis after spending a week aboard. They may assume pumps, air conditioners, and heads all sound like they are barely hanging on because they have no better reference point. Even crew can fall into the same trap if all they have known is less-than-stellar boats. Once dysfunction becomes familiar, people start calling it normal.

It is not normal.

That is one of the harder parts of experience. You stop hearing just noise and start hearing symptoms. You stop seeing a beautiful boat and start noticing the signs that the systems underneath it are tired, neglected, or already in decline. That is also when the captain’s life gets more difficult, because eventually you have to tell an owner that something major needs to be addressed, and the answer you often get back is, “It’s always worked that way.”

Until it doesn’t.

A lot of expensive surprises aboard yachts are only surprising to the people who got used to the warnings.

Checklists matter because they replace assumption with accountability.

Having procedures means we get to operate knowing something is ready, not just hoping it probably is. That distinction sounds small right up until it isn’t.

That is true across the boat, but safety gear makes the point especially well. Lifejacket checks become repetitive, and because they are repetitive, people start treating them as automatic. The assumption becomes that the number of lifejackets in the locker has not changed because nobody has used one, moved one, borrowed one, or left one in some other compartment. But that is still just an assumption.

Actually opening the locker, visually checking them, and counting them does something important. It turns “they should be there” into “we know they are there.” It also confirms they are not tangled up, buried, damaged, or otherwise not ready to use when the time comes.

That is the real value of a checklist.

It is not there to create paperwork. It is there to force someone to verify reality before reality gets a vote. In a real emergency, you do not want to be untangling lifejackets, discovering one is missing, or deciding in real time who does not get one. By then the checklist has already failed, and so have the people responsible for it.

Good procedures are not about looking organized. They are about removing guesswork from the moments when guesswork becomes expensive.

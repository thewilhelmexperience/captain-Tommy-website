MSD and blackwater systems are some of the first things I worry about when joining a new boat, because they are also some of the first things previous crews tend to avoid until avoidance is no longer an option.

That pattern has repeated itself often enough that I no longer treat service logs as proof that the system is healthy. On both *Lady B* and *Lady Victory*, the blackwater system ended up giving me a very direct education in what these systems actually need in order to function properly, how quickly small neglect turns into a disgusting operational problem, and how often people convince themselves that if the toilets still sort of work, the system must be fine.

It usually is not.

Nobody likes dealing with human waste. That is exactly why these systems get neglected. They are unpleasant, out of sight when they are behaving, and easy to postpone in favor of more visible jobs. But ignoring the system that handles waste is not a strategy. It is just a delayed emergency.

At this point, when I join a boat, blackwater is one of the systems I want eyes on early, no matter what the paperwork says. The first things I want checked are the transfer pump, the hoses, and the chemical pump and reservoir. I also want a proper dose of the correct tank treatment to make sure the biology is balanced from the start. Those are first-step items every time.

I want to know what has actually been serviced, what has only been claimed, what is close to failure, and what bad habits have been normalized around it. If you wait until the boat is full, the guests are aboard, or the tank is at capacity, you are already late.

The lesson is simple: if the waste system looks like something nobody wants to think about, that is probably because nobody has been thinking about it.

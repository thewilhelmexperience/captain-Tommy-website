We departed Erie Canal Lock 23 at 0648 on September 23, 2021 aboard a Hatteras 75 Panacera, expecting this to be part of the home stretch into Troy and the final eastbound push on the Erie Canal.

But the canal had other ideas.

The previous five days had brought so much rain to the region that several sections were at flood stage. Around the locks, the dams were pushing huge amounts of water, and many of the downstream exits were far more turbulent than normal. Conditions for the day itself were not especially dramatic, mid-70s, overcast, light winds, but the canal system was clearly still carrying a lot more water than usual.

Even so, most of the day went smoothly.

The problem came just after Lock 8.

We approached a bridge we had passed under without issue on the westbound trip earlier that spring. As usual, the mate was in position on the ladder checking overhead clearance. Instead of the expected all-clear, he called down that we did not have room, and not by a little. We were somewhere around six to eight inches too tall for the bridge in the canal’s current state.

With current running and no interest in learning that lesson the expensive way, I brought the boat as close as I was comfortable to confirm it was real and not an optical illusion.

It was real.

We called the canal authority and reported the estimated clearance issue. They created an alert, and we turned around, locked back through Lock 8, and tied up on the calm side above the lock wall. After that, there was not much to do except let the system catch up.

So we secured the boat, talked with the lock master for a while, and then rode our electric scooters to the nearest restaurant for a decent lunch. Later we rode down the bike path to the bridge itself and took a few pictures of the water level. The lock master guessed they would have the canal dropped enough by around 0900 or 1000 the next morning.

At 0800 the next day we rode back to check it again and could already see how much difference the canal authority had made. It was actually impressive to watch that system do exactly what it was designed to do. We waited another hour just to be safe, then got underway again.

This time we had a little over two inches to spare.

We only made 74 nautical miles that day, and the canal bought itself one more day with us than we had planned on giving it. But it also reinforced a lesson that is easy to get lazy about in familiar water: procedure matters every time. It did not matter that we had already passed under that bridge months earlier. The mate checked clearance like he was supposed to, and that saved us from a very embarrassing and very expensive mistake.

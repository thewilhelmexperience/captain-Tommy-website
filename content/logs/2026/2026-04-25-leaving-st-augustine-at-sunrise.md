We were off the dock at St. Augustine at 5:45 this morning and headed for Charleston before the day was fully up.

By 6:08 we were out St. Augustine Inlet and turning north outside. The sun was coming up on another beautiful day, with a light one- to two-foot swell on the starboard quarter and the boat settling into an easy 17-knot run.

There are worse ways to start a day than that. So far it is another comfortable northbound leg with Charleston Safe Harbor ahead.

By 10:30 we were still running along nicely. The wind had clocked around and was knocking down the small seas we started with, but the following swell was putting a shudder through the boat that would both improve and get worse depending on whether the stabilizers were engaged or centered. It is one of those fiddly underway issues that does not feel completely natural, and my suspicion is that it may trace back to the all-glass enclosure added to the flybridge, or possibly the add-on hydraulic swim platform.

A little later the problem started to make more sense. We determined that the swim platform drags and the rudders do not engage adequately below about 15 knots. Backing down to 15 knots in the following seas took most of the shudder out of the boat, which at least gave us a workable answer. So the plan from there was simple enough: hold 15 knots and carry that into Charleston.

Even then the boat was still surfing now and then. The following swell would occasionally kick us up to 18 or 19 knots, but without the harmonics that had been rattling the flybridge, all while still turning 1750 rpm. Outside had gone hazy and there was that unmistakable smell of something burning ashore.

The radio also turned into a reminder of why the Coast Guard matters. We started hearing traffic about a person aboard a 26-foot boat roughly 50 miles east of Jacksonville who had suffered a heart attack. There was the usual confusion that comes when messages are being relayed through several different voices at once, with people trying to pin down exactly where the boat was and what the patient’s condition actually looked like. Even without hearing every side of the exchange, it was obvious the Coast Guard was handling it cleanly and professionally, and it sounded like they were also coordinating with the local sheriff while a few concerned boaters nearby helped relay what they could.

Before we ran out of range, we heard that a helicopter had been dispatched and that they were passing patient vitals while the crew worked to transfer him aboard. Listening to that unfold and hearing it handled so well does make you feel just a little safer being offshore. Semper Paratus.

The day ended in Charleston. We pulled into Charleston City Dock at 6:04, got fueled, and tied off for the night. After a long day offshore, that was enough.
